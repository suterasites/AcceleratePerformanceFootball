Position-Specific Training: Why a Striker and a Defender Train Differently

14 October 2026 5 min read APF Coaching Team

Football training that treats every position the same stops improving a player past a certain point. A striker and a centre-back are doing almost entirely different jobs on the pitch. Their technical requirements differ, the scenarios they train for differ, and the physical demands specific to their role differ. The players who develop fastest are the ones whose coaching reflects that reality.

Generic Training Has a Ceiling

Most group training sessions - whether junior development programs or senior club sessions - use exercises that apply broadly to every player on the field. Rondos, possession drills, shooting practice, small-sided games. These sessions have value. They build shared fundamentals and give players game-like exposure. But they stop being specific after a point. When every player in a drill is working on the same skill, no one is working on the particular skill their position demands most.

The gap shows up in match performance. A striker who spent the off-season doing possession drills has improved their ball retention but may have done almost nothing to sharpen the movement that creates finishing opportunities. A defender who has been practising long distribution has improved one aspect of their game but has not touched the defensive shape and recovery runs that keep them solid under pressure. Generic work has a ceiling and most players hit it without knowing why their improvement has slowed.

What a Striker Actually Needs to Train

At the core of a striker's technical game is finishing under pressure and movement before the ball arrives. In private sessions we focus on body orientation as a striker receives in the box, the ability to turn and finish in one motion, and the timing of runs to get goalside. Striking cleanly from different angles, with both feet, under simulated pressure, is a skill that requires specific and repeated practice. It is rarely covered in depth in group training environments.

Equally important is the technical side of pressing. Modern football asks strikers to work defensively. The angle of a press, knowing when to trigger and when to hold, and how to cut off passing lanes by positioning the body correctly - these are technical details that separate strikers who only attack from strikers who are useful for ninety minutes.

What a Defender Actually Needs to Train

Defenders are dealing with a different set of problems. The technical priorities are positioning and recovery - getting goalside, holding a line, reading when to step and when to drop, making the decision to tackle or jockey. These are skills that are difficult to practise in a group session because they require one-on-one confrontation and tactical setup that generic drills do not replicate well.

For players defending in Melbourne's local competitions, the physical side of defending also gets specific attention. Acceleration over short distances, the ability to recover quickly after a mistake, and the body strength to hold position under pressure all require conditioning that is specific to what a defender faces in a match, not just general fitness work applied to every player equally.

The Midfield Difference

Midfielders sit between both worlds and have their own distinct demands: pressing triggers, covering ground, transitioning between attack and defence quickly, and distributing accurately under pressure from multiple angles. Position-specific training for a central midfielder looks different again from a winger, who might focus on driving at defenders, delivering wide balls with accuracy, and shifting back into a defensive shape quickly when possession is lost. Even within the same positional line, the role shapes the training.

How Position-Specific Work Fits Private Coaching

The advantage of one-on-one coaching is exactly this specificity. In the Senior Refinement program at APF, the first session begins with an assessment of where a player currently sits technically and physically. From there, the training plan targets the gaps that are specific to the player's position. A winger working on crossing accuracy. A centre-back working on stepping out to intercept. A striker working on second-phase movement after an initial chance is missed.

In our sessions, players get focused individual attention on the techniques their role demands. That specificity is what makes improvements visible quickly: when the drill matches the exact scenario a player faces in their position, the gap between practice and match performance closes fast. Over 125 athletes have trained with us across Melbourne and the difference between position-specific work and general group training is consistent - players stop hitting the ceiling.

  • Strikers: finishing mechanics, movement timing before the ball arrives, and pressing triggers are the core technical priorities
  • Defenders: positioning, recovery runs, the decision to tackle or jockey, and body strength under pressure shape their training focus
  • Midfielders and wingers have their own demands again - distribution under pressure, transition speed, and role-specific movement patterns
  • General group training builds shared fundamentals but rarely reaches the technical depth that a specific position requires
  • Private coaching allows the plan to mirror the exact scenarios and decisions a player faces in their role, session after session
A training plan that treats a striker and a defender the same is not developing either of them as well as it could. Position shapes every rep.

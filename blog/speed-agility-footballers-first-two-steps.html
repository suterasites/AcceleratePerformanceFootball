Speed and Agility for Footballers: Training the First Two Steps

30 September 2026 5 min read APF Coaching Team

In football, most decisive moments - a run behind the defensive line, recovering to block a cross, getting a yard on your marker before the ball arrives - are settled in the first two steps. Top-end pace matters, but it rarely decides games. The ability to accelerate hard from a standing or moving start, in any direction, is what separates players at every level.

Why the First Two Steps Matter More Than Top Speed

Football is a game of repeated short efforts. Most sprints in a match cover fewer than fifteen metres - often fewer than ten. What matters is not how fast a player runs at full speed, but how quickly they reach that speed from rest. A player who can accelerate sharply off both feet, in tight quarters, with a defender close by, is harder to handle than a player with better top-end pace who takes four or five strides to get there.

This is why dedicated speed and agility training for footballers looks different from general athletics work. The goal is not a faster hundred-metre time. It is a faster first two steps in a game-realistic context - reacting to a ball, moving off a cue, changing direction under pressure.

Speed and Explosiveness: Understanding the Difference

A common mistake in football training is treating speed and explosiveness as the same quality. They are related but distinct. Speed is the ability to maintain fast running over distance. Explosiveness - often called acceleration or first-step quickness - is the ability to generate force rapidly from a static or moving position. Most footballers need significantly more work on explosiveness than raw speed, because that is the physical quality that matches actually test.

Explosive acceleration is trained through short, high-intensity efforts with full recovery between reps. The nervous system needs to learn to fire the right muscles, in the right sequence, as hard as possible. That adaptation does not come from long runs or high-volume conditioning work. It comes from deliberate, focused repetitions with quality prioritised over quantity.

The Mechanics of a Fast First Step

Before adding volume to acceleration training, a player needs to understand what a good first step looks like mechanically. The key elements are a low body angle at the start of the sprint (driving forward rather than running upright), a powerful toe-off from the back foot, and arms that drive aggressively to assist the initial thrust. Players who stand tall and shuffle into their sprint consistently lose ground to players who know how to load and drive out of a starting position.

This is a coachable skill. Most players have never had it broken down for them. Once the movement pattern is clear, acceleration improves noticeably - not because the player has become fitter, but because they are applying the strength they already have more effectively.

Training Agility Alongside Speed

Agility - the ability to change direction quickly and efficiently - is a different adaptation from straight-line speed. It requires deceleration mechanics (how you slow down before changing direction), ground contact time, and the ability to read a situation and react to it. In football, most agility is reactive: a player responds to a teammate's movement, a defender's position, or the ball's path. Training that includes reactive cues transfers to games more directly than pre-programmed cone patterns alone.

A well-structured speed and agility program for a footballer covers several distinct areas:

  • Short acceleration drills (under 10 metres): building explosiveness and first-step power from standing, moving, and prone starts
  • Direction change patterns: working on deceleration mechanics and the ability to reload and accelerate out of a turn
  • Reactive agility work: responding to visual or auditory cues so the nervous system learns to fire quickly under game-like pressure
  • Plyometric work: bounding, single-leg hops, and jump variations that build the elastic strength underpinning fast movement
  • Technical overlap: ball-carrying speed, acceleration onto a through-ball, and movement patterns that match position-specific demands

Why the Off-Season Is the Best Window for This Work

Speed and agility adaptations require intensity. They are not compatible with a fatigued body or a match-prep mindset. The off-season is the window where a player can train these qualities at the doses they actually require - frequent, high-intensity sessions with full recovery between them - without the constraint of upcoming fixtures pulling the focus back to maintenance.

During the season, a coach's priority is keeping a player fresh for match day. Genuine acceleration work, at the intensity it needs to be to produce adaptation, rarely fits that window. The off-season removes that constraint. A player can sprint at maximum effort, recover fully, sprint again, and do that repeatedly across a structured block of weeks.

How APF Approaches Speed and Agility Training

Off-season conditioning at APF is built around individual assessment. We look at how a player accelerates, where they lose ground in direction changes, and what movement patterns are limiting their speed on the ball. That picture drives the program - not a generic drill menu applied to every player regardless of what they actually need.

We work with players across Melbourne. In a one-on-one setting, every repetition gets real-time feedback on position, drive angle, and ground contact - the technical details that make the difference between a drill that produces change and one that reinforces the same habits.

Where to Start

The best time to begin this work is now, early in the off-season, so there are enough weeks to build a meaningful adaptation before preseason camps begin. A player who addresses speed and agility in October arrives at preseason in November noticeably sharper than they left last season - not slightly better, but genuinely different in how they move.

Getting scouted: what selectors actually look for

25 November 2026 5 min read APF Coaching Team

Most players who attend trials or representative assessments have done the work. They show up fit, they know the game, and they want the spot badly. But the players who get picked are rarely the ones who tried hardest on the day. They are the ones who looked like they had already solved the problems the selector was testing for. Understanding what those problems are and training toward them specifically is what separates a player who gets noticed from one who goes home without a call-back.

What selectors are actually watching

Selectors at every level have limited time and a specific mandate. They are not watching 22 players and hoping the best one stands out. They are running through a checklist of qualities that the team or program needs, and they are looking for which players reliably demonstrate those qualities. The first filter is not talent. It is consistency. A player who does the right thing most of the time is more valuable to a selector than a player who does something brilliant twice and then disappears.

Physical qualities are noted - work rate, recovery speed, aerial presence for those in certain positions - but selectors with experience tend to discount raw athleticism quickly if it is not paired with footballing intelligence. A fast player who consistently reads the wrong run is a liability. A composed player who is never out of position is an asset. The assessment is about reliability under the specific conditions of the game, not an isolated attribute test.

Technical qualities that catch the eye

First touch is the first technical signal. A player who controls the ball cleanly and immediately puts themselves in a position to do something useful is demonstrating trained technique. A player whose first touch knocks the ball too far, or who has to take an extra step to adjust, is signalling that the basics are not yet automatic. Selectors notice this within the first few minutes because it happens on every single touch.

Beyond first touch, the technical checklist varies by position. For a midfielder, it is passing range and the ability to receive, turn and release quickly. For a wide player, it is 1-v-1 delivery and the courage to take on a defender. For a central defender, it is clean distribution under pressure and decision-making in defensive transition. Knowing which technical attributes are being assessed for the position you are trialling for is not insider knowledge. It is basic preparation.

The mental qualities selectors log

Scanning is one of the clearest signals of footballing intelligence, and it is visible from the touchline. A player who checks their shoulder before receiving the ball is processing information before the ball arrives. That means their decision is made earlier, their body shape is better, and their next action is faster. Selectors at higher levels look for this deliberately because it predicts performance under pressure better than technical ability alone.

Response to mistakes is another. Every player makes errors in a trial. What separates candidates is what happens immediately after. A player who drops their head, loses focus, or starts playing cautiously is demonstrating a mental habit that will create problems in a competitive team environment. A player who resets immediately and continues to play their game is showing resilience that is genuinely difficult to coach, and therefore highly valued by selectors at every level.

The gap most players do not close before trialling

The majority of players who miss out at trials are not lacking in effort or desire. The gap is usually the same: their technical habits are inconsistent under pressure. What they can do in a relaxed training environment does not hold up when the pace lifts, the opposition is tighter, and the decision window is smaller. Closing that gap requires deliberate repetition at higher intensity than most club sessions deliver.

  • First touch consistency when receiving at pace or under a pressed body
  • Passing accuracy when the target is marked and the time window is tight
  • Scanning frequency when moving into receiving positions
  • Body shape and footwork when receiving to turn versus receiving to hold
  • Decision-making speed in 1-v-1 and 2-v-1 situations
  • Recovery shape and work rate without the ball

Training with selection in mind

The most effective preparation for a trial is not replicating the trial environment. It is building the specific habits that the trial will test, so they run automatically when the pressure is on. That means identifying which qualities matter for your position and your target level, then drilling them at an intensity that forces those habits to hold under stress. This is different from general training. It requires a clear picture of where the gaps are and sessions structured to close them.

APF's Senior Refinement program is built for exactly this kind of targeted preparation. Sessions are structured around the specific technical and tactical qualities that matter for the position and standard the player is targeting. The goal is not to make players look good for one game. It is to build habits that hold up consistently - which is what any selector at any level is ultimately trying to find.

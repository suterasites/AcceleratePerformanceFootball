Small-group vs 1-on-1 coaching: which is right for your player?

18 November 2026 4 min read APF Coaching Team

The most common question parents and players ask when they first contact APF is whether they should book 1-on-1 sessions or join a small-group program. It is a fair question because both options are on offer and the difference is not obvious until you have trained in both formats. The honest answer is that both work, but they work in different ways and are best matched to different situations. Getting the choice right from the start means faster progress and less wasted time.

Two different environments, two different outcomes

1-on-1 coaching puts every minute of a session on one player. The coach watches, corrects and challenges that player continuously. There is no waiting in line, no standing-around time, no compromise on what gets drilled. A session built for a teenager who needs to improve weak-foot passing under pressure looks nothing like a session built for a younger player who is still forming their first touch - and in a 1-on-1 environment, that specificity is fully achievable.

Small-group coaching typically involves two to four players working together. That changes the dynamic in ways that are sometimes an advantage and sometimes not, depending entirely on what the individual player needs to fix.

What 1-on-1 coaching delivers

The core benefit of 1-on-1 is focused repetition. A player who needs high volumes of a specific skill - weak-foot passing, receiving at pace, turning under pressure - gets exactly that, with direct feedback on every single touch. There is no dilution of the session and no waiting while another player goes through a rep. Progress is fastest when the problem is technical and the solution is a habit that needs to be built through sheer volume of correct repetition.

1-on-1 also suits players with a specific goal that is time-sensitive. A player returning from injury who needs to rebuild movement confidence, a senior player preparing for trials, or a junior making the step up to a higher standard - all benefit from the undivided attention that only 1-on-1 provides. When time is short and the target is clear, 1-on-1 is the most direct route.

What small-group coaching delivers

The defining advantage of small-group coaching is real-time interaction with another player. Passing combinations, pressing patterns, decision-making under pressure from a live opponent - these things are very difficult to replicate in a pure 1-on-1 environment. A player who is technically solid but struggles to apply their skills when someone is actually in front of them needs the small-group format. The improvement in that case is not a technical one; it is a decision-making one, and decision-making only sharpens against a real player.

There is also a competitive element in small groups that some players respond to strongly. Working alongside a peer at a similar level creates natural motivation. Players who train better when there is someone to push against often make stronger gains in a group setting than they would in an equivalent number of 1-on-1 sessions.

Age is worth factoring in too. Younger players often build faster in 1-on-1 because individual attention accelerates habit formation at the stage when habits are still being laid down. Teenagers who are already established in a team environment tend to respond well to either format, though many find small-group sessions more engaging because the competitive element keeps attention sharp throughout.

The question is not which is better - it is which fits right now

Both formats are legitimate training methods. The question that determines which is right for a specific player is not about preference - it is about what that player actually needs to change right now. That answer is different for every player and can shift across a season.

  • Weak technical habits that need intensive correction: lean toward 1-on-1
  • Decision-making or in-game application problems: lean toward small-group
  • An upcoming trial or deadline with a fixed date: 1-on-1 is usually the faster route
  • A player who already trains at a club and needs focused top-up work: either format works
  • A player who is not currently in a team environment: small-group adds live interaction that solo training cannot replace

When combining both makes sense

Many APF players cycle through both formats over the course of a season. A common pattern is 1-on-1 sessions through the off-season to address specific technical deficiencies, then small-group work in the preseason to sharpen decision-making with a live opponent. That sequencing is deliberate: build the technical foundation first, then apply it in a context that tests it. Players who try to skip the technical work and go straight to group application usually find that the same habits show up under pressure.

How APF approaches the question

When a new player gets in touch via the contact page, the first step is a short conversation about where the player is now and what they want to change. That conversation determines the starting format. There is no default recommendation for one over the other - the right starting point follows from what the player actually needs. If 1-on-1 is the right fit, that is what gets recommended. If small-group is the better match, the player joins the next available group.

APF has been running both formats in Melbourne for over four years, working with more than 125 players across age groups and levels. That breadth means the starting recommendation is grounded in what has actually worked for similar players, not a guess.

  1. Get in touch with the player's age, position and the main thing they want to fix
  2. APF comes back with a recommended format and a clear starting point based on what you describe
  3. The first session sets a baseline - from there, the program adjusts based on what actually shows up in training

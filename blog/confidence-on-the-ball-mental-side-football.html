Confidence on the ball: coaching the mental side of football

4 November 2026 5 min read APF Coaching Team

Confidence on the ball is not a personality trait. It is a skill, and like all skills, it can be coached. Junior players who look hesitant, who pull out of challenges, who play the safe pass rather than the right one, are not lacking courage. They are lacking the technical foundation that makes bold decisions feel possible. Building that foundation is the starting point for every confident footballer.

What confidence on the ball actually looks like

A confident player controls the ball under pressure without tensing up. They receive the ball facing the right way, make a decision before it arrives, and execute that decision at pace. They back themselves to beat a defender, to play out of pressure from their own half, to try a skill in the right moment. These are not personality traits. They are technical habits built through deliberate practice.

Why junior players lose confidence

Group training environments can undermine confidence without anyone intending it. When a player makes a mistake in front of peers, the instinct is to pull back, to play it safe, to avoid trying the skill again. Over time, that self-protection becomes a pattern. The player gets better at avoiding risk, not at managing it. Confidence built on avoiding mistakes is fragile. Confidence built on executing skills correctly is durable.

The other confidence-killer is technical uncertainty. A player who is unsure of their touch, not confident in their weak foot, or unsure of their body position when they receive the ball will hesitate. That hesitation shows up as anxiety, as poor decision-making under pressure, as the feeling of freezing on the ball. The fix is not encouragement. It is correct repetition.

The technique-confidence loop

Confidence and technique are not separate things. They feed each other. A player who can execute a skill correctly under moderate pressure will trust themselves to try it in a harder moment. Each successful rep reinforces that trust. Each poorly executed rep chips away at it. This is why drilling fundamentals correctly, at the right pace and in the right context, is not just technical work. It is confidence work.

The loop runs both ways. As confidence grows, players attempt more difficult moves, receive in tighter spaces, and take on challenges they would previously have avoided. Those attempts generate new reps, and new reps generate new technical gains. Coaches who understand this do not just teach skills. They manage the conditions under which skills are practised, and they progress players through difficulty at the right rate.

Coaching confidence deliberately

Building confidence requires more than positive feedback. It requires a structured progression. Players need to master a skill at low pressure before they encounter it at high pressure. When the progression is managed correctly, players arrive at the difficult situation already believing they can handle it, because they have handled easier versions of it many times. That prior success is not luck. It is the product of a planned process.

  • Drill skills to automaticity in isolated settings before adding pressure or defenders
  • Celebrate correct process, not just successful outcomes, so players learn what they did right
  • Progress deliberately: add one variable at a time, speed first, then pressure, then decision load
  • Use repetition counts rather than time blocks so a player knows exactly how much practice they are completing
  • Replay successful skill sequences with the player so they build a mental picture of what good looks like

What parents can do

Parents have more influence on a player's confidence than most realise. The most important thing is reinforcing effort and process over result. A player who hears 'you kept backing yourself today' after a tough match leaves the ground more confident than one who hears a correction about a specific decision. The former builds self-belief. The latter, delivered at the wrong moment, can build hesitation for next time.

It also helps to manage expectations around skill acquisition. Learning a new touch technique or body movement takes time. Players who understand that they are building a skill, not failing until they get it right, approach the development process differently. They have more patience with themselves, and that patience translates directly to greater willingness to attempt and repeat the things that need improving.

Private coaching and the confidence environment

The environment in which a player trains has a direct impact on their confidence. In a Junior Prep session, the structure is built around the individual: progressions are set at the right level, mistakes are coached immediately without an audience, and successful reps are built into sequences the player can carry into matches. That is different from a group setting where the pace is set by the median, not the individual.

Players who train one-on-one or in small groups are not being sheltered from competition. They are building the technical foundation that lets them compete with more confidence when they get there. The skills practised privately are the ones trusted publicly. What a player can do easily in training is what they will attempt under pressure in a match. That relationship between private preparation and public performance is what confidence actually is.

Confidence is built in training, rep by rep. The player who backs themselves on match day is the player who backed themselves in every session before it.

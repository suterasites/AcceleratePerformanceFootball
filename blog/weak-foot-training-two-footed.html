Weak foot training: how to become genuinely two-footed

23 September 2026 4 min read APF Coaching Team

Most footballers have a dominant foot and a foot they avoid. That is normal at junior level. What is not acceptable is arriving at senior football still hiding from your weak side. Defenders read your patterns inside the first five minutes. Once they know you always check back to your right, they close the angle and leave you with nothing. The players who commit seriously to weak foot development become genuinely harder to read - and significantly more effective in tight situations.

Why most players never fix it

The standard approach is to tack a few weak-foot touches onto the end of a session when you are already tired. A ball rolled to the left, a few clumsy passes, and you call it done. That is not training - that is exposure. Real weak foot development requires structured repetitions early in a session, when your neuromuscular system is fresh and capable of learning new movement patterns. Done correctly, this is not about kicking with the wrong foot. It is about building a second set of motor pathways that can perform under match pressure.

Four drills worth doing consistently

These drills work because they isolate the weak foot, keep the movement football-specific, and progress in difficulty. They suit players from age 10 upward and can be done in any open park with a ball and a wall or a set of cones.

  • Wall passes, weak foot only: 3 sets of 30 passes at a wall 5-7 metres away. Keep the reps fast and accurate. Sloppy reps reinforce sloppy patterns - if the return is coming back awkward, slow down and reset your body shape before the next touch.
  • Inside-of-the-foot passing over 20 metres: Set two cones 20 metres apart. Walk the distance once, then jog it, focusing on contact point - mid-inside of the foot, plant foot beside the ball. The goal is not power. It is repeatability.
  • Turn and drive: Place a ball 5 metres in front of you, approach it at pace on the weak side, control it with the inside of the weak foot while turning, and drive it 10 metres. The challenge is the transition - most players break down when the control and the direction change happen together.
  • Wide crossing from the weak side: From a wide angle, take 3-4 touches to build pace and cross into a target zone. Use a cone or bag as a target if you are training alone. The goal is generating enough pace and accuracy that the delivery is usable, not just reached.

What realistic progress looks like

Six to eight weeks of two deliberate weak-foot sessions per week produces noticeable improvement in control and confidence - enough that you will stop actively avoiding the foot. Three to six months of consistent work narrows the gap significantly. You are not trying to match your dominant foot exactly. You are trying to reach the point where you have a usable second option: a pass you can play, a touch you can trust under light pressure.

Once you reach that point, the game opens up. Options you used to ignore become available. Angles you used to close down stay open. Defenders who had your pattern memorised have to recalibrate every time they press you.

How structured coaching accelerates the process

Training the weak foot alone is hard because the feedback loop is slow. You know something felt wrong but you may not know why - and without correction, the bad pattern reinforces itself across hundreds of reps. In a private session, a coach can diagnose the specific breakdown: whether it is body shape on approach, ankle lock, contact point, or weight transfer. That correction happens in real time rather than six weeks later, when the wrong movement is already habitual.

Junior Prep at APF is specifically structured to address technical gaps like this one. Players across Melbourne come to our sessions knowing each hour is built around their individual needs - including, when it matters, spending a serious block of time on the foot they would rather avoid.

A two-footed player is not twice as hard to defend. They are exponentially harder to read.

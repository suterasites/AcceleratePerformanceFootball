5 first-touch drills you can practise at home

29 July 2026 5 min read APF Coaching Team

A poor first touch kills your momentum before the move has started. A great one gives you half a second of space that changes everything. The good news: first touch is one of the most trainable skills in football, and you do not need a full pitch or a training partner to get better at it. These five drills can be done solo, at home or at your local park, and they transfer directly to game situations.

Why first touch matters more than most players realise

Every time you receive the ball you are making a decision in under a second: where does this touch go, and what do I do next? Players who control well under pressure buy time and space. Players who miscontrol concede possession or draw pressure they cannot escape. In our sessions we see this pattern constantly - a technically capable player who struggles under pressure almost always has a weak first touch at the root of the problem. Fix the touch, and other parts of the game open up.

Drill 1: wall passing with a target zone

Stand 4-6 metres from a solid wall. Pass the ball at the wall with your dominant foot, then control the return with your non-dominant foot - directing the ball into a small target zone you have marked with two cones or water bottles about a metre apart. The goal is not just to stop the ball but to set it into that zone ready for your next action. Start slowly, increase the pace of your pass as your control improves. Do three sets of 20 touches per foot.

This drill builds the habit of directing your touch rather than just killing it. When you have a specific zone to hit, your body starts to automatically angle the receiving surface - inside of the foot, outside, or sole - rather than just bracing for contact.

Drill 2: drop-and-control from height

Hold the ball at chest height with both hands and drop it. As it rises from the first bounce, control it with the inside of your foot and direct it forward two metres. Reset and repeat. This simulates the awkward bouncing balls that come off a goalkeeper's clearance or a long punt. Progress the drill by adding a half-turn before the control so you are receiving from behind your body - exactly as defenders and midfielders need to do in match play.

Drill 3: juggling with purpose

Juggling for a high score is a party trick. Juggling with purpose is a different thing. Set yourself a sequence: right foot, left foot, right thigh, chest, head, then bring it back down to your feet. The moment the ball drops, reset. The objective is a clean, controlled sequence - not volume. This trains body awareness, timing, and the soft touch needed to set the ball exactly where you want it. Players around the western suburbs who stick with this drill for six weeks almost always report noticeably cleaner aerial control in matches.

Drill 4: first touch and turn

Place two cones 1.5 metres apart. Stand behind one cone facing the wall. Pass to the wall, then receive the return and control the ball past the second cone in a single touch - turning 90 degrees. This is the foundation of the half-turn that allows midfielders to receive under pressure and immediately open up play in a new direction. Vary the receiving surface: inside foot, outside foot, sole roll. The ability to change direction on first touch is one of the clearest separators between players who progress and those who plateau.

Drill 5: pressure reps

Set a timer for 60 seconds. Pass to the wall continuously as fast as you can while keeping clean control of every return. Count your miscontrolled touches - anything that goes outside a 1-metre radius around you counts as a miss. Keep a weekly log of your miss count at the same pace. This drill trains your touch under fatigue and time pressure, which is when technique always breaks down first. A player who can keep their miss rate low in the final 15 minutes of a match has genuinely internalised the skill.

Putting the drills into a session

  • Warm up with 5 minutes of light jogging and dynamic stretching before starting
  • Do drills 1 and 2 first - they are the most technique-focused and benefit from fresh concentration
  • Move to juggling sequences as a transition - it keeps the session flowing without spiking intensity
  • Finish with drill 4 and drill 5 - first touch and turn builds the pattern, pressure reps stress-tests it
  • Keep total solo work to 30-40 minutes maximum; quality drops off sharply after that
  • Log one metric each session - miss rate, number of clean sequences, or wall pass pace - so progress is measurable

How solo work fits into structured coaching

Home drills build repetitions. Structured coaching builds the right repetitions. Without a coach watching, players often repeat the same compensation patterns - leaning back, using the wrong part of the foot, receiving on the wrong angle - without knowing it. The volume goes up, but the bad habit becomes more ingrained. At APF, we assess a player's first touch in the opening session and build a technical plan around what we see. Players across Melbourne come to us for exactly this: targeted feedback that home practice alone cannot give you.

These five drills work best as preparation for your next Junior Prep or private session - not as a replacement for one. Use them to get more reps between sessions, and you will notice the difference quickly.

First touch is not talent. It is a pattern your body has practised until it became automatic. Train the right pattern enough times and it shows up when the pressure is highest.

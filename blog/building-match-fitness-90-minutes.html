Building match fitness that lasts 90 minutes

28 October 2026 4 min read APF Coaching Team

Playing 90 minutes of football is not just about fitness - it is about the right kind of fitness. Players who run out of fuel in the final 20 minutes, or who lose sharpness after a high-intensity spell, are not necessarily unfit by general standards. They are missing match-specific conditioning. Building the capacity to perform at your best from the first whistle to the last is a different challenge, and one that requires a deliberate, structured training approach.

What match fitness actually means

General cardio helps, but it does not replicate the specific demands of football. A match involves repeated short sprints, recovery jogs, lateral movements, jumps, and physical contacts, spread unevenly across 90 minutes. Some spells are quiet. Others compress high-intensity moments back to back with almost no recovery time. Your body needs to handle both ends of that spectrum.

Match fitness means three things working together: your aerobic engine is large enough to sustain effort across the full game, your anaerobic capacity is high enough for repeated explosive bursts, and your recovery speed is fast enough to back up those efforts without accumulating fatigue. Training one without the others leaves gaps that show up in the second half.

Build the aerobic base first

Without a strong aerobic base, everything else is harder. Aerobic capacity determines how quickly you clear lactate between efforts, how efficiently your heart delivers oxygen to working muscles, and how long you can sustain intensity before your decision-making deteriorates.

Continuous running at moderate intensity, steady-state tempo runs, and longer interval sessions at a controlled pace all develop this base. Footballers often skip this work because it feels too slow or too easy. That is a mistake. The aerobic base is what allows you to recover between sprints, not just survive the quiet phases of the game. You cannot shortcut it with fast, short work alone.

Layer in the explosive work

Once the aerobic base is established, you layer in the work that mirrors match conditions: short, intense efforts with incomplete recovery. High-intensity interval training, repeated sprint training, and game-scenario circuits stress the same energy systems football demands.

The key word is repeated. Running one fast sprint is not the challenge. Running your 15th sprint in the 70th minute, when your legs are already heavy and your lungs are burning, is where match fitness separates players. Your training needs to put you in that state regularly so your body learns to perform there.

Drills that build repeated sprint capacity

  • 10 x 30m sprints with 30 seconds recovery between each
  • 6 x 200m efforts at 85-90 per cent of max pace, 90 seconds recovery between reps
  • 4 x 3-minute pressure circuits (pass, move, press, repeat)
  • Small-sided games with high work-to-rest ratios (5 minutes active, 2 minutes passive)
  • Ladder intervals: sprint 20m, jog 20m, sprint 30m, jog 30m, sprint 40m, jog back

Train for the final 20 minutes

The last 20 minutes is where matches are won and lost, and where the fitness gap between teams becomes visible on the scoreboard. Most players train for the start of a match. Fewer train specifically for the end of one.

One method: finish every session with 15 to 20 minutes of high-intensity work when your body is already fatigued. Ball circuits, sprint intervals, or possession-based games played at the back end of a demanding session teach your body to stay sharp when it hurts. This is a specific physiological adaptation, not general fitness. It can only be built by training that way, repeatedly, over weeks.

What the off-season window gives you

Players who use the off-season to build a genuine fitness base arrive at preseason in better shape than their teammates expect. During the season, match fitness is maintained. In the off-season, it is built. Those are different training states, and the off-season is the window where structural gains actually happen.

Footballers who train through the break with a structured off-season conditioning program start the season physically ahead. Not marginally ahead - genuinely ahead in aerobic capacity, sprint recovery, and the physical resilience to back up a full 90 minutes from round one.

The two common mistakes

Most players do too much of one thing. Either they run long and slow, building an aerobic base without ever developing the explosive capacity football requires, or they hammer short-burst interval work without the aerobic foundation to support it. Match fitness requires both systems trained and working together.

The second mistake is treating weekly matches as training. Playing once a week is not enough on its own to build or maintain match fitness, particularly in youth football where match volume can be limited. Structured sessions between games, targeting the specific energy systems football taxes, are where real development happens across a season.

Fitness built in the off-season is the kind that lasts the full game. In-season you maintain it. In the off-season you build it.

Scanning: The Habit That Separates Good Players From Great Ones

2 September 2026 4 min read APF Coaching Team

If you watch elite players closely, you notice something before the ball even reaches them: they are already looking. A quick glance to the left. A check over the right shoulder. Head up, information gathered, decision already made before the pass arrives. That habit is scanning, and it is one of the clearest technical dividers between players who react and players who act.

What Scanning Actually Means

Scanning means checking your surroundings before you receive the ball. Not after. Not as it arrives. Before. You are gathering information - where your markers are, where space exists, where your teammates are positioned - so that when the ball lands at your feet, you already know what you are doing with it.

It sounds simple. Most players understand the concept the first time it is explained. The gap is not understanding - it is habit. Scanning needs to become automatic, something that happens without thinking, not a checklist item you remember when the game slows down. Getting there requires specific, repeated practice with that trigger deliberately built in.

Why It Changes the Game

The difference in decision speed is significant. A player who scans before receiving the ball can play a first-time pass or turn immediately. A player who scans after receiving is already a beat behind - the space has shifted, the defender has closed in, the option has gone.

For players in Melbourne - whether stepping up from juniors or working on your senior game - this is consistently one of the areas that separates players who look comfortable on the ball from players who always seem to be scrambling. The ball speed at senior level punishes the habit of waiting to see before deciding. When the game moves faster, the player who has not scanned is always late.

The Senior Level Shift

The jump from junior football to senior competition exposes this gap quickly. Junior football often allows time on the ball. Players can receive, settle, look up, and then decide. At senior level, the press comes faster, the windows close sooner, and the margin for settling before deciding shrinks considerably.

Players who have not built the scanning habit often describe the step up as overwhelming - the game feels faster, touches feel rushed. But a lot of what feels like lack of fitness or lack of touch is actually a lack of information. The player is receiving blind and then trying to solve problems in real time that could have been solved two or three seconds earlier. Sorting the scanning habit out often changes how a player perceives the pace of senior football more than any other single adjustment.

Building the Habit Through Repetition

Scanning is not trained by talking about it more. It is trained through deliberate, structured repetition. At APF, we use specific passing patterns and movement sequences where the trigger to scan is built directly into the drill. Before the ball is played, the player checks. Every time. Over dozens and then hundreds of reps, that timing gets wired in as the default response.

The work we do in Senior Refinement sessions focuses on exactly this - taking a player who already understands the game and layering in the technical habits that make decision-making faster and more consistent. Scanning is usually near the top of that list, because the return on focused practice is immediate. Players who arrive uncertain about when and how often to check see measurable improvement within a short run of sessions.

Common Mistakes Players Make

Most players have some version of scanning in their game already. The issue tends to be in the execution.

  • Scanning too late - checking as the ball arrives rather than two or three seconds before, so the information is already outdated by the time you use it
  • Scanning in one direction only - always checking the same side becomes a predictable pattern that sharp defenders will read and time against you
  • Scanning without acting on it - gathering information and then still taking an extra touch to settle before deciding, burning the advantage the scan created
  • Dropping the head after the scan - looking up once and then watching the ball all the way in, losing the picture you just built before the ball has arrived

What You Are Actually Training

In a session focused on scanning, we watch the exact moment a player's head moves in relation to the ball. Small adjustments to timing - scanning two seconds earlier, adding a second check on the opposite side - create noticeable shifts in how composed a player looks and how quickly they move the ball. After a focused block on this, players often describe the game as feeling slower. The game has not slowed. The player is simply arriving at each moment with more information already in hand.

That is the goal. Not a technique applied consciously mid-match, but a rhythm that becomes part of how a player moves and positions before every touch. The players who do it well look like they have more time. They do. They bought it with their eyes a few seconds earlier.

The best players are not making faster decisions in the moment. They made the decision before the moment arrived. That is what scanning gives you.
